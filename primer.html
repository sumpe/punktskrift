1 The cell

Braille is read by touch, a few millimetres at a time. Every character sits in a cell: two columns of three dots, about 2.5 mm apart, small enough that one fingertip covers the whole cell at once.

The dots are numbered down the left column, 1 2 3, then down the right, 4 5 6. Braille readers and teachers name every character by its dots: d is “dots 1 4 5”. Each dot is either raised or flat, so a cell has 64 possible patterns. Leave out the blank one and that leaves 63 for letters, digits, punctuation and everything else.

Try it: tap the dots

2 The alphabet's pattern

The braille alphabet isn't 26 shapes to learn one by one. It's ten shapes, used three times.

The first ten letters, a to j, use only the top four dots. The next ten, k to t, are the same shapes with dot 3 added. The last row adds dots 3 and 6 to the first five shapes, giving u v x y z.

Try it: point at a letter to see its row-mates

Ringed dots are the ones each row adds to the first.

And w? Louis Braille built the alphabet in French order, and in the France of the 1820s w was seldom used. So it doesn't fit the pattern: it comes after the others, on dots 2 4 5 6. That is j with dot 6 added.

The same trick gives the digits: a to j double as 1 to 9 and 0. There's more on that in the next section.

3 Capitals, numbers, signs

63 patterns can't cover 26 letters twice over (small and capital), ten digits and all the punctuation. So braille reuses its cells and puts a sign in front that says how to read what follows. These signs, called indicators, take up a cell of their own but have no print letter.

Punctuation reuses the first ten shapes too, moved down a row. In English, a comma is a's dot moved down to dot 2, and a full stop is d moved down to dots 2 5 6. That's why reading braille is partly reading where in the cell the dots sit.

4 Every country its own braille

Braille isn't a language. It's a way of writing languages, and each country fits it to its own. The letters a to z are the same nearly everywhere, but letters like å, ł and ç, the capital sign and even the full stop are each country's own choice.

5 Where it came from

In the early 1800s, the school for blind children in Paris taught reading from books printed in large raised letters, the method of its founder Valentin Haüy. The books were heavy, and the letters were slow to feel out.

In 1815 Charles Barbier, who had served in the French artillery, published a writing made of raised dots for blind people. His cells held up to twelve dots in two columns, and they spelled out the sounds of French rather than its letters. It was later nicknamed “night writing”, and for a long time people said he had made it for soldiers to read in the dark. His 1815 book shows he meant it for the blind from the start.

Louis Braille was born on 4 January 1809 in Coupvray, a small town east of Paris. At three he hurt his eye with an awl in his father's harness workshop; the infection spread, and by five he was blind in both eyes. In February 1819 he went to the Royal Institute for Blind Youth in Paris, where he met Barbier's dots. While still a pupil, at about fifteen, he began reworking them: six dots instead of twelve, standing for letters instead of sounds.

In 1829 he published his Method of Writing Words, Music, and Plainsong by Means of Dots, for Use by the Blind and Arranged for Them. That first version still mixed dots with dashes; the second edition, in 1837, used dots alone. Music was part of it from the start.

Braille died in Paris on 6 January 1852, aged 43. Two years later his own school adopted his system. A hundred years after his death his remains were moved to the Panthéon in Paris. Today there are braille codes for well over a hundred languages.

6 How braille is written

Slate and stylus

The oldest way, and still the cheapest, is a slate, a hinged frame with rows of cell-shaped windows, and a stylus, a blunt point. You press each dot into the back of the paper, so the bumps rise on the other side. That means you write starting at the upper right, each cell backwards, and turn the sheet over to read it.

The Perkins Brailler

A braille typewriter has one key per dot, and you press the keys for a cell all at once. The best known, the Perkins Brailler, was designed by David Abraham at the Perkins School for the Blind and first sold in 1951. It has six dot keys, a space key, a backspace key and a key for a new line, and well over 300,000 have been made. The Drill's Write mode uses the same finger layout on a computer keyboard: F D S for dots 1 2 3, J K L for 4 5 6.

Embossers and displays

A braille embosser is a printer that punches braille into heavy paper, often on both sides of the sheet. Braille embossing machines go back to the end of the 1800s.

A refreshable braille display sits beside a computer keyboard: a row of cells, usually 40 or 80, whose round-tipped pins rise and fall to show a line of text, often driven by piezo crystals that grow when a voltage is applied. Each cell has eight pins: computers add dots 7 and 8 beneath the six, making room for 256 patterns. Unicode has all 256 in its Braille Patterns block, U+2800 to U+28FF, added in version 3.0 in 1999. Those are the characters the Press copies when you press Copy braille.
