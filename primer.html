How braille works, from six dots to a whole page.

1 The cell

Braille is read by touch, a few millimetres at a time. Every character sits in a cell: two columns of three dots, about 2.5 mm apart, small enough that one fingertip covers the whole cell at once.

The dots are numbered down the left column, 1 2 3, then down the right, 4 5 6. Braille readers and teachers name every character by its dots: d is “dots 1 4 5”. Each dot is either raised or flat, so a cell has 64 possible patterns. Leave out the blank one and that leaves 63 for letters, digits, punctuation and everything else.

Try it: tap the dots

2 The alphabet's pattern

The braille alphabet isn't 26 shapes to learn one by one. It's ten shapes, used three times.

The first ten letters, a to j, use only the top four dots. The next ten, k to t, are the same shapes with dot 3 added. The last row adds dots 3 and 6 to the first five shapes, giving u v x y z.

Try it: point at a letter to see its row-mates

Ringed dots are the ones each row adds to the first.

And w? Louis Braille built the alphabet in French order, and in the France of the 1820s w was seldom used. So it doesn't fit the pattern: it comes after the others, on dots 2 4 5 6. That is j with dot 6 added.

The same trick gives the digits: a to j double as 1 to 9 and 0. There's more on that in the next section.

3 Capitals, numbers, signs

63 patterns can't cover 26 letters twice over (small and capital), ten digits and all the punctuation. So braille reuses its cells and puts a sign in front that says how to read what follows. These signs, called indicators, take up a cell of their own but have no print letter.

The capital sign says the next letter is a capital: in braille it's . A doubled or separate sign covers a whole word in capitals: .

The number sign, , turns a to j into the digits 1 to 0 until the word ends, so 2026 is the number sign followed by b j b f. If a letter from a to j follows a number, as in 3a, the letter sign says the number is over.

Punctuation reuses the first ten shapes too, moved down a row. In English, a comma is a's dot moved down to dot 2, and a full stop is d moved down to dots 2 5 6. That's why reading braille is partly reading where in the cell the dots sit.
